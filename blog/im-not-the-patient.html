What Does Support Look Like When I'm Not the Patient?

November 2, 2026

When someone you love is struggling with addiction, mental illness, a disability, or a condition that comes with aging, it can be difficult to know where you fit into the picture.

You’re probably the person coordinating appointments, managing responsibilities, helping with daily tasks, or putting on a brave face so that your loved one feels comfortable. You might spend most of your day thinking about that person, wondering what to say, or trying to figure out what to do next. And somewhere along the way, your own feelings and needs may have slipped to the bottom of the list.

When someone else is the one receiving treatment, it can be easy to assume that your role is simply to be supportive. But you can care deeply about someone else's well-being while also needing support of your own.

You can be affected without being the patient.

When a loved one is struggling, the effects rarely stay contained to one person. Dementia reverses the natural role of caregiver between parent and child. A partner's addiction affects your trust, finances, parenting, and a sense of stability. A family member's mental illness can bring uncertainty, fear, and difficult decisions into the home. A disability or chronic condition can reshape daily routines and long-term plans.

You might experience grief over the changes in your relationship, even while that person is still here. You might feel frustrated by responsibilities you never expected to carry, guilty for wanting a break, or resentful that so much of your life revolves around someone else's needs.

You might also feel relieved when a loved one receives treatment or additional care, only to feel guilty for that relief.

These feelings don’t mean that you don’t also feel love, compassion, and a genuine desire to help your loved one. Having complicated emotions does not mean you care any less. It means you're a person experiencing a difficult situation, too.

What does support actually look like?

Support for you doesn’t have to mean abandoning your loved one or finding a miracle to help with their situation. Support might just mean having a place where you can acknowledge what you're experiencing without having to manage someone else's reaction.

Having space for your own feelings. You may need somewhere to express anger, sadness, fear, exhaustion, or uncertainty without worrying about burdening your loved one. If they’re not in a headspace to have that conversation, therapy can offer that space.

Learning to set boundaries. You can show that you care about someone without being expected to be on call 24/7, ignoring your own needs for theirs. Boundaries can help to determine what time should reasonably be yours, so that you can be fully there when they need you.

Navigating difficult decisions. You may be weighing whether a parent needs more care, how to respond to a partner's addiction, or how to balance a loved one's needs with your responsibilities to your children, work, or yourself. Often times, the people close to us have their own opinions about these choices and you may not have someone who will be truly unbiased to help you weigh the pros and cons of these decisions, determining which choice will actually lead to an outcome you’re okay with.

Understanding changes in your relationships. Your role in a family may have changed. You might feel more like a caregiver than a daughter, more like a supervisor than a partner, or more like a provider than an equal family member. Talking through these changes can help you understand what you need from your relationships moving forward.

Finding ways to care for yourself that are realistic. We can’t all do self care that involves a spa weekend, or turning off your phone for the night, or going on a shopping spree. But we can find ways for you to practice realistic self-care that feels natural for yourself, even if it’s just for 20 minutes each day, to help remind you that you haven’t gotten lost in your loved one's situation.

Therapy can help with all of these conversations. It isn't about convincing you to stay in a difficult relationship, leave one, provide more care, or step away from your loved one. Therapy is about making a space for yourself, a space to understand your circumstances, consider your options, and make choices that align with your needs and values.

You can love someone and still need something for yourself.

People often seek support only after they feel they can no longer keep up. They might tell themselves that their loved one's situation is more serious, or that asking for help would be selfish. But support isn't a competition, and your distress doesn't have to be greater than someone else's to matter. On an airplane, we’re instructed to put on our own oxygen mask before we can help someone else. Caring for yourself isn’t selfish, it’s a requirement before you can do the same for others.

There may not be a simple solution to what your family is experiencing. Your loved one may continue to struggle, their needs may change, or the future may remain uncertain. But you can begin to recognize how those circumstances are affecting you, what support you need, and how you want to care for yourself along the way.
